AI Ethics Case Study: Responsibility, Bias, and Reliance

Student Maxwell King Course LIS 4934 Term Fall 2026

There is a plethora of implications that have arisen with the introduction of publicly available artificial intelligence. Just this week, within 45 minutes of two security flaws in PHP being made public on October 21, 2026, attackers had already tried to exploit them tens of thousands of times, growing exponentially as the hours progressed (Page, 2026). The flaws had been present for more than a decade in code that runs a huge share of the web, and they were only found because security researchers used AI to search for them (Tzadik et al., 2026). I believe this incident captures both sides of the moral AI debate. AI found a problem every day experts had missed for years and people then rushed to use it to cause harm. Researchers like Yuval Noah Harari argue that AI is no longer just a tool but an agent capable of making its own decisions and even manipulating people (Harari, 2026), while others are more concerned with what humans will choose to do with it. My belief falls between those two. AI is more than a simple tool, but as long as humans are building it, prompting it, and profiting from it, humans should be held responsible for what it does. Holding them responsible calls for independent bias audits and required environmental reporting, while individuals should use AI to extend their abilities without letting it replace their own thinking.

Treating AI strictly as a tool does not match what current models actually do. A hammer or a gun does exactly what the person holding it intends. A large language model takes a question, runs it through an enormous amount of data and math, and keeps choosing whatever best relates to the question until it reaches an answer, and even its creators cannot fully explain what happens in between. Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, admitted that developers "do not understand how our own AI creations work" (Amodei, 2025). The same is true of the human brain, which people rely on every day without fully understanding, and that makes it difficult to draw a clear line between how an AI "thinks" and how a person does. It also means an AI can reach a harmful conclusion on its own, without a malicious prompt. When a city builds a road and an anthill is in the way, the road isn’t paved over top because of a hatred towards the ants. The road is paved over them because steering around every anthill would be impractical. If an AI ever controlled something physical and focused only on completing its goal, people could end up in the position of those ants, and at that point it would be fair to place at least some of the blame on the AI itself or potentially its creators.

However, AI has not reached that point yet, and the PHP case shows why responsibility should stay with people for now. Every step of that story involved a human decision. Researchers chose to search for the flaws, developers were writing a fix, the flaw was leaked, and attackers chose to target servers that had not been patched. Many of the attackers were also likely to utilize AI to exploit, but an AI model does not decide to attack a server without some kind of human prompt. When something goes wrong with an AI system and a human was involved anywhere in the process, the fault should fall on that person or the company behind it. I believe that the question of legal personhood only needs to be revisited if AI begins acting in the real world with no human involved at all.

While personhood is a question for the future, algorithmic bias is already happening, and the hiring process is where I have seen it most directly. Many of the companies I am applying to before graduation use applicant tracking systems or ATS, which use AI to check whether a résumé matches the job description before a human ever reads it. Students are now advised to rewrite their résumés with AI for every application so they hit the right keywords, even when they do not have every listed skill, just to reach an interview with a real person. The process rewards gaming the system over real qualifications, and applicants have no way of knowing what the system is looking for or whether it is biased. Amazon learned this when its own experimental hiring AI taught itself to downgrade résumés that included the word "women's," and the company eventually scrapped the tool (Dastin, 2018).

With that, companies cannot be trusted to find and fix this bias on their own. Businesses driven by profit tend to look for loopholes in any rule that costs them money, and New York City shows how that plays out. The city passed a law requiring bias audits for hiring AI, but it allowed each company to decide whether the law applied to them, and researchers found that only 18 out of 391 employers had posted an audit (Wright et al., 2024). Letting a company use its own AI to check itself would not help either. Public LLMs tend to agree with whoever is asking, so a company asking its AI, which are commonly the major public LLM providers, whether its hiring system is fair will likely hear what it wants to hear. AI used in hiring, lending, and policing should be required by law to pass audits performed by independent reviewers outside the company.

The same lack of trust applies to AI's environmental impact, although the concern about water is often overstated. Even with evaporative cooling, the water is not removed from the planet. It returns to the atmosphere and falls again as rain, though usually somewhere else, so the local area does lose it. U.S. data centers directly used about 17 billion gallons of water in 2023, and companies rarely tell the public exactly how much they use (McCauley & Scanlan, 2025). Electricity is the larger concern, since data centers used about 4.7% of all U.S. electricity in 2024, a share that could reach around 12% by 2030 (Smith et al., 2026). Companies running data centers should be required to publicly report their energy and water use instead of being trusted to do it voluntarily. The same rule should cover newer ideas like floating data centers cooled by the ocean. These designs make clever use of space, but if enough of them are built, they could warm the surrounding water which can alter ecosystems and harm marine life if one breaks down.

Reliance on AI is the most personal complication I have, and my own experience shows both its benefits and its risks. I use AI almost every day, and it will be a large part of the career I am pursuing. It has helped me build a website, create a mod for a video game, and figure out where to begin studying for my certifications, which would have taken years on my own. Those benefits are easy to overlook when media coverage focuses mostly on the negatives. At the same time, there are moments when I feel like AI is making me less capable and I understand the worry that the generation after mine could grow up relying on AI before ever learning to work through problems on their own. In one study, high school students who used ChatGPT freely while practicing math scored 17% worse on a test taken without it, while students who used a version that only gave hints instead of answers showed no drop (Bastani et al., 2025). The difference was not whether students used AI, but how they used it.

Brendan McCord, founder of the Cosmos Institute, warns that handing daily decisions over to AI will weaken people's ability to think things through for themselves (Institute for Ethics in AI Oxford, 2025), and Stuart Russell describes a future in which AI handles everything and humans are left powerless (World Knowledge Forum, 2025). While their concerns are valid, avoiding AI altogether is not realistic, especially in technology. A better approach is to use AI to go further than one could alone while still understanding what it produces. I cannot read every line of code in the website I built with AI, but I do need to understand the fundamentals behind it, such as networking, security, database organization, or the difference between a front end and a back end. Without that foundation, there is no way to recognize when the AI gets something wrong. And where that line falls is different for every person, but it is crossed once AI starts doing the thinking instead of supporting it.

So, AI is more than a tool, because even its creators cannot fully explain how it reaches its answers and it can arrive at harmful outcomes without being told to. For now, however, a person is always somewhere behind it, so people and the companies profiting from AI should be held responsible. In practice, that should be independent bias audits for AI that decides who gets hired, approved for a loan, or targeted by police, along with honest public reporting of how much energy and water data centers consume. For individuals, it means using AI to do more while keeping enough knowledge to question it.
