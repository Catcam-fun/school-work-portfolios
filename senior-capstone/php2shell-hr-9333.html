PHP2Shell and H.R. 9333: AI-Found Flaws and Federal Policy

Student Maxwell King Course LIS 4934 Term Fall 2026

PHP is a programming language behind about 70 percent of websites, so a serious flaw in PHP itself can affect a large part of the internet at once (Association for Computing Machinery [ACM], 2026). In October of 2026, researchers using AI found two flaws in PHP, now called PHP2Shell, and many governments and companies took their websites offline until they could patch (ACM, 2026). AI has made finding bugs a lot faster, but fixing them still falls on small, underfunded teams, and the federal bug database can’t keep up. The bill I traced, H.R. 9333, is a good start, but it deals with flaws inside AI rather than flaws AI finds, and it doesn't do much for the people writing the fixes.

PHP2Shell is made up of two CVEs, CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. A CVE, refers to a list of common vulnerabilities or exposures, and is the public ID number for a known security bug. These bugs are in PHP itself rather than in any one website, and they let an attacker run their own code on a web server without logging in (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency [CISA], 2026). The PHP Foundation (2026) rated both flaws 9.2 out of 10 for severity. The AI help came from an OpenAI audit program that began this summer and an earlier scan using Anthropic's Claude Mythos (PHP Foundation, 2026). The PHP Foundation says AI helps check and group reports, but people still make every decision and work on every patch (PHP Foundation, 2026). The fix for these specific vulnerabilities came out on October 21st, 2026. CISA told organizations to find every system running PHP, patch right away, and take systems offline if they could not patch. It also warned that PHP often comes built into other software, so some parties may not know they even run it. On October 26th, CISA added both flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list, which means attackers were already using them (CISA, 2026).

To me, the core issue is that finding bugs is no longer the hard part, but instead that fixing them is. In 2024, while working on findings from an audit funded by Germany's Sovereign Tech Agency, a PHP developer found early signs of the memory problem behind one of these flaws. No one found a working attack at the time, and other issues took priority (PHP Foundation, 2026). After that, the security team could not agree on a fix, as every option had trade-offs, the team made decisions by consensus, and only a few people understood that part of the code. The Foundation blames the delay on its own decision process and lack of resources, and it is moving to majority votes (PHP Foundation, 2026). Since July, the same team has published 15 CVEs and sorted through more than 100 reports that were invalid, duplicates, or could not be reproduced (PHP Foundation, 2026). On the government side, NIST announced in April that it would only add full details, like severity scores, to priority entries in the National Vulnerability Database (NVD). And while submissions have kept growing, its staff stayed at 21 people (Greig, 2026).

When I looked through H.R. 9333, I found the AI Flaw Reporting and Security Enhancement Act, introduced in June by Rep. Deborah Ross with one cosponsor from each party. It would have NIST, working with CISA, run a voluntary program for reporting and tracking flaws in AI systems. This would include common definitions, severity rankings, norms for when AI flaws should be made public, and a national AI flaw database, either new or built on an existing one. The House Science, Space, and Technology Committee voted 35 to 0 to advance it on June 25, and that is still the latest action listed (AI Flaw Reporting and Security Enhancement Act, 2026). The Congressional Budget Office (CBO, 2026) estimates it would cost about $36 million from 2026 to 2031, mostly for the database and staff, and expects NIST to build on the NVD rather than start from scratch. Which is questionable, since the NVD is the same database NIST just had to scale back. The overarching problem is that the bill is about flaws in AI systems, while PHP2Shell is about flaws found by AI in ordinary software. So even if it passed tomorrow, it wouldn't help the PHP maintainers who wrote these patches.

However, where the bill and this event do have similarities is disclosure to the public. A bug like this is normally kept quiet, usually through an embargo, which is an agreement to keep quiet until the fix is ready so attackers do not get a head start. This particular embargo fell apart and on October 20th, the CVE numbers leaked early and a researcher's video was posted by mistake. Chainguard, a company that builds software packages, then put out fixed versions more than six hours before the planned release, which the Foundation says ignored weeks of coordinated work (PHP Foundation, 2026). Jay Gazlay, a CISA official, said Chainguard's move dangerous but Chainguard's CEO defended it and asked CISA to help create guidance for when releasing early is acceptable (Doubleday, 2026). That kind of guidance is pretty much what H.R. 9333 asks NIST to come up with, but only for AI flaws.

The people doing the actual fixing are the PHP Foundation and other open source maintainers, and they are asking companies, hosting providers, cloud platforms, and governments for steady funding (PHP Foundation, 2026). AI and security companies find the bugs, so they also help decide when the bugs go public. CISA then puts out the warnings, NIST runs the NVD, and Congress decides how much of it gets funded. Then there are the organizations running PHP, some of which don't even realize it, the people whose data sits on those sites, and attackers, who can use the same AI tools to do harm.

I think AI finding bugs is a good thing overall, at least in this scenario since defenders found these first. But if there aren't enough people to fix what AI finds, it just pushes the problem down the line. Congress or a body similar, should fund critical open source projects directly and set clear disclosure rules so the next Chainguard situation is not a judgment call. H.R. 9333 should still pass, but it should cover flaws found by AI too and come with real funding, so the new work doesn't get dumped on the NVD's small team of 21 people.

For information professionals, the biggest lesson is that patch windows are shrinking, and you can't patch what you don't know you have. For society, this showed that public websites can go dark over code most people have never even heard of, and that a big part of the web depends on a pretty small group of underfunded maintainers.

AI has sped up the finding side of security, but the fixing side hasn't yet caught up, and H.R. 9333 only partly closes that gap. The current Congress ends on January 3, 2027, so I'd like to see the bill pass before then, along with real support for the people maintaining critical software. If it does not, someone will have to introduce it, or something like it, again in the next Congress.
